# Investment philosophy

I want to own a handful of businesses I'd be comfortable holding for 15 years or more. The target is 15% a year over rolling five-year periods. I won't hit it every year, but it's the standard I hold myself to. In order of priority, I look for: the highest-quality companies with wide and preferably expanding moats; high-quality companies the market misunderstands and misprices; and companies critical to a supply chain or theme with a clear edge over competitors.

**The business comes first.** I look for capital-light businesses with high and improving ROIC and pricing power. The ideal one raises prices without losing customers, grows without proportionate reinvestment, and becomes more rather than less valuable as time passes.

## Moats I look for

Every moat has to defend against two threats, an idea I took from an interview with Chris Hohn: substitution, where a different product or technology replaces the need, and competition, where a rival does the same thing better or cheaper. The strongest moats handle both and last for decades.

1. **Irreplaceable asset or IP complexity.** No amount of capital or time can replicate it, because the asset is fixed or the know-how took decades to build. It covers both threats, so it's the most durable moat. Examples: ASML's EUV lithography; the S&P 500, part of an index family with $27.7T of assets indexed or benchmarked to it.
2. **Regulatory or institutional mandate.** The customer has no real choice, because regulation or market convention requires the product. Examples: FICO scores in US consumer lending; credit ratings, which investor mandates and insurance and bank capital rules require in practice.
3. **Proprietary data that can't be replicated.** Datasets built over decades that a competitor can't rebuild from zero. AI should make that data more valuable, but only the data is protected, not the software seats around it. Examples: S&P's private credit pricing and evaluated bond data; Uber's traffic and ride data.
4. **Network effects at scale.** Each new participant makes the network more valuable, so a challenger falls further behind over time. The strongest form is two-sided. Examples: Meta's social graph; Mastercard's cardholders and merchants.
5. **Installed base and switching costs.** The first sale creates a long tail of recurring revenue, and replacing the product costs more than switching could ever save. Examples: AWS workloads; FICO's software on top of its scores.
6. **Brand and distribution.** The weakest on its own. Brand only counts when it comes with pricing power, distribution scale or an ecosystem. Examples: Google Search; Amazon Prime.

Scale alone isn't a moat I rely on. A big company without network effects or IP is easier to disrupt and has less pricing power.

The most durable businesses have more than one moat. ASML has IP complexity and an installed base, Mastercard has network effects and switching costs, and S&P Global has an irreplaceable asset, a mandate, proprietary data and switching costs. I count them, because a single-moat business needs a bigger valuation discount.

**Then the price.** The price already contains a forecast, so a great business already priced for greatness isn't an edge. I start with what the price requires, using a reverse DCF, and only buy when I have a defensible reason the outcome will be better. I've learned not to buy quality at any price: every entry has to pass a strict margin of safety check. The return I count on comes from compounding, meaning FCF per share growth plus the dividend at a constant multiple. A re-rating is a bonus. Buybacks at or below intrinsic value are one of the easier ways to get above-average returns, so I care how management spends its cash.

**Then how I'd know I'm wrong.** Each thesis gets three or four invalidation triggers, each a metric with a threshold that I check on a schedule, plus a one-line pre-mortem. I don't sell on macro fears, price drops alone, short-term earnings noise or sentiment.

## Applied to S&P Global

| Principle | SPGI test | What I measure |
|---|---|---|
| Moats, counted by segment | Ratings: mandate in practice (investor mandates, capital rules) + switching costs on outstanding debt. Indices: irreplaceable asset (S&P 500; $27.7T indexed or benchmarked to S&P Dow Jones Indices, Dec 2024). Platts: prices written into commodity contracts. Market Intelligence: proprietary data, and the AI-exposed piece | Barriers per segment and what would erode each |
| Substitution vs competition | AI is a substitution risk for parts of Market Intelligence, not for the benchmarks | MI organic growth and retention |
| Pricing power | Ratings earns a 67% margin, and about 77% on each extra dollar of revenue (9M 2026) | Ratings margin and incremental margin |
| The price already contains a forecast | What growth does today's price require, and what do I expect? | Reverse DCF implied growth vs my estimate |
| Don't buy quality at any price | Does the base case clear the hurdle with a margin of safety? Does the bear case still earn a positive return? | Base and bear IRR vs hurdle |
| Return comes from compounding | ~4% of shares retired a year on top of organic growth | FCF/share CAGR, buyback yield |
| Buybacks at or below intrinsic value | Is management buying back stock below what it's worth? | Buyback price vs intrinsic value |
| Invalidation triggers | MI organic growth below 3% for three quarters; an issuance downturn; Indices fee compression; a serious capital allocation mistake | Checked each quarter |

## Tensions I have to defend

- Indices fees move with markets and Ratings moves with issuance, which sits awkwardly with owning businesses that don't depend on cycles. My answer: surveillance fees are recurring, maturities of bonds issued since 2019 peak in 2029–31, and both businesses have grown through full cycles.
- "Why SPGI over Moody's?" The multiple gap, similar AI exposure, and benchmarks (Indices, Platts) that Moody's doesn't have.
