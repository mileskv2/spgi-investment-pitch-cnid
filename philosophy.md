# Investment philosophy

I want to own a handful of businesses I'd be comfortable holding for 15 years or more. The target is 15% a year over rolling five-year periods. I won't hit it every year, but it's the standard I hold myself to.

**The business comes first.** I look for capital-light businesses with high ROIC and pricing power, protected by a moat that's deeply embedded in their industry. The ideal one raises prices without losing customers, grows without needing much reinvestment, and becomes more rather than less valuable over time. The moats I trust most are regulatory or institutional mandates, proprietary data that can't be replicated, irreplaceable assets, network effects and deep switching costs. Brand on its own is the weakest, and scale alone isn't a moat I rely on.

**Every moat faces two threats.** Substitution, where something else replaces the need, and competition, where a rival does the same thing better or cheaper. The best moats handle both. I count them, because a business with one moat needs a bigger discount than a business with three.

**Then the price.** The price already contains a forecast, so a great business already priced for greatness isn't an edge. I start with what the price requires, using a reverse DCF, and only buy when I have a defensible reason the outcome will be better. I've learned not to buy quality at any price: every entry has to pass a strict margin of safety check. The return I count on comes from compounding, meaning FCF per share growth plus the dividend at a constant multiple. A re-rating is a bonus. Buybacks at or below intrinsic value are one of the easier ways to get above-average returns, so I care how management spends its cash.

**Then how I'd know I'm wrong.** Each thesis gets three or four invalidation triggers, each a metric with a threshold that I check on a schedule, plus a one-line pre-mortem. I don't sell on macro fears, price drops alone, short-term earnings noise or sentiment.

## Applied to S&P Global

| Principle | SPGI test | What I measure |
|---|---|---|
| Moats, counted by segment | Ratings: mandate (NRSRO) + switching costs on outstanding debt. Indices: irreplaceable asset (S&P 500, ~$20T benchmarked). Platts: prices written into commodity contracts. Market Intelligence: proprietary data, and the AI-exposed piece | Barriers per segment and what would erode each |
| Substitution vs competition | AI is a substitution risk for parts of Market Intelligence, not for the benchmarks | MI organic growth and retention |
| Pricing power | Ratings is roughly 3% price + 4% volume a year, at 66–72% margins | Price vs volume split, incremental margin |
| The price already contains a forecast | What growth does today's price require, and what do I expect? | Reverse DCF implied growth vs my estimate |
| Don't buy quality at any price | Does the base case clear the hurdle with a margin of safety? Does the bear case still earn a positive return? | Base and bear IRR vs hurdle |
| Return comes from compounding | ~4% of shares retired a year on top of organic growth | FCF/share CAGR, buyback yield |
| Buybacks at or below intrinsic value | Is management buying back stock below what it's worth? | Buyback price vs intrinsic value |
| Invalidation triggers | MI organic growth below 3% for three quarters; an issuance downturn; Indices fee compression; a serious capital allocation mistake | Checked each quarter |

## Tensions I have to defend

- Indices fees move with markets and Ratings moves with issuance, which sits awkwardly with owning businesses that don't depend on cycles. My answer: surveillance fees are recurring, refinancing comes due heavily in 2027–28, and both businesses have grown through full cycles.
- "Why SPGI over Moody's?" The multiple gap, similar AI exposure, and benchmarks (Indices, Platts) that Moody's doesn't have.
